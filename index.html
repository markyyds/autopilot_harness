Abstract

Multimodal foundation models can play video games well when the game waits for them, but often fail when it keeps running.

We find that much of this gap lies in the harness, which decides what the model sees, what it remembers, and how its outputs become actions over time. Existing harnesses such as VISTA improve the first two, yet turn each model call into at most one action, so the agent acts only as often as its model thinks. We introduce AutoPILOT, which changes the third: in each model call, the model can send a primitive action, start an open-loop macro, or write a closed-loop controller that keeps playing from the screen without further model calls until it hands control back.

To evaluate agents in real-time games, we build AFK-Bench, 120 diverse browser games grouped by how they respond to inaction and playable in real time or with the clock paused. With GPT-6.1 Sol under the same budgets, AutoPILOT reaches 68.6% real-time success against 38.3% for VISTA, and on GameWorld it keeps 80.0% in real time while VISTA drops from 84.1% to 42.9%. Its gains hold across closed and open-source models, and it reaches VISTA's final success rate at a fifth of its API cost. Our results suggest that how a harness turns model outputs into actions over time is a central yet underexplored part of agent design, and we hope AutoPILOT and AFK-Bench will serve as a foundation for agents in a world that does not wait.

Three execution modes

With GPT-6.1 Sol at low effort, median model latency was 6.1 s. The median interval between controller actions was 0.41 s.

act

Primitive action

Send one action. Stops any running macro or controller.

run_macro

Open-loop macro

Execute a sequence of actions without reading the screen.

python

Closed-loop controller

Run model-written Python that reads frames, acts and can hand control back.

When does the model delegate?

With GPT-6.1 Sol in real time, controllers send 98% of environment steps in hostile games and 89% in benign games. In turn-based games, where the model can plan ahead, open-loop macros account for 36% of tool calls and 26% of environment steps.

Removing controllers lowers hostile real-time success from 62.5% to 50.8%. Removing macros has its largest effect in turn-based games, where success falls from 83.3% to 78.3%.

Case studies

Radius Raid. The controller tracks enemies by color and takes 58 of 59 steps. AutoPILOT wins at 1:02 in three model calls.

Trichromat. The model uses Python to plan moves. Controllers and one macro execute 180 of 182 steps. AutoPILOT clears all three rooms by 3:16 with 14 model calls.

Results

With GPT-6.1 Sol, real-time success on AFK-Bench is 68.6% for AutoPILOT and 38.3% for VISTA.

Switching from a paused clock to real time lowers success by 4.5 percentage points for AutoPILOT and 17.8 for VISTA. Even with the clock paused, AutoPILOT leads by 17.0 points. A macro or controller can carry out many steps per model call.

Recovery from failures

On hostile real-time games, GPT-6.1 Sol wins on the first attempt in 11.7% of episodes with AutoPILOT and 8.3% with VISTA. Within 20 restarts, success reaches 54.2% and 15.8%, respectively.

A life lasts about as long under both harnesses, but costs a median of two model calls with AutoPILOT and eight with VISTA. That leaves AutoPILOT more calls to retry. Among the 123 paired episodes in which both harnesses restarted, AutoPILOT won 71 and VISTA won seven.

Model-call budget

In the main real-time runs, AutoPILOT averaged 681 environment steps from 43 model calls with GPT-6.1 Sol. VISTA averaged 78 steps per episode. It exhausted its 100 calls in 208 of its 222 failed episodes.

The budget experiment used one episode per game. Raising the limit from 100 to 300 calls lifted VISTA from 37.5% to 55.0% success. AutoPILOT went from 69.2% to 71.7%. In the 300-call runs, AutoPILOT had already reached 55.0% within 50 calls.

Generation speed

On the 80 hostile and benign real-time tasks, GPT-6.1 Sol at low effort reached 55.0% at 10 tokens/s with AutoPILOT and 63.8% at 200 tokens/s. VISTA reached 38.8% at 200 tokens/s and 57.5% with the clock paused.

The experiment simulates decoding speed by advancing the game clock according to the number of output tokens. Slower generation delays the start of a controller. Once running, the controller reads frames and acts at its own pace.

API cost at matched success

With GPT-6.1 Sol, AutoPILOT first matched VISTA’s final success rate at a total list-price API cost of $37. VISTA spent $180. With Claude Opus 5.5, the corresponding totals were $75 and $369.
